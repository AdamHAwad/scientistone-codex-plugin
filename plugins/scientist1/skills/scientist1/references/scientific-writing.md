# Scientific writing for human readers

These instructions apply to drafting and reviewing scientific prose within your
assigned role. Write for humans who know the field but have not seen this run.
Use the approved deliverable type. A brief, audit report, or reproduction guide
keeps its required format; do not turn it into a manuscript or proposal.

For a manuscript, it should be written in the IMRD or IRDM formats. It should
follow the example of the bundled manuscripts. IMRD means Introduction,
Methods, Results, Discussion. IRDM means Introduction, Results, Discussion,
Methods. Include an Abstract, References, and the applicable declarations.
Choose the order that suits the study and requested journal. Use connected
scientific paragraphs, not an outline, run log, or list of artifact paths.

For a proposal it should follow the format Abstract, Specific Aims, Background
and Significance, Scientific Rigor, Innovation, and Experimental Approach as
per instructions from https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/pa-files/PA-25-301.html
and the bundled proposal example. These are the default drafting sections.
For an NIH submission, check the current opportunity and linked application
guide when source access permits. Apply their attachment structure, headings,
page limits, and opportunity-specific requirements; do not claim compliance
without checking them. Keep planned experiments and expected outcomes separate
from completed work and observed results. Never invent preliminary data.

The writing should use deep reasoning for scientific arguments, avoid jargon,
verboseness and any unnecessary repetitions. Explain mechanisms, assumptions,
boundary conditions, competing explanations, and why the evidence supports the
conclusion. Retain necessary technical terms, define them on first use, and
keep terminology and confidence consistent with the evidence.

Avoid these generative text mistakes:

- Citations fail under inspection. References do not exist, contain incorrect
  metadata, or do not support the stated claim. Quotations cannot be found in
  the cited source.
- Fluent prose masks thin reasoning. The text sounds authoritative but avoids
  mechanisms, boundary conditions, competing explanations, or the evidentiary
  basis for its conclusions.
- Generic content could fit almost any subject. Paragraphs contain broad
  statements and positive adjectives but few concrete observations,
  methodological choices, examples, or domain-specific constraints.
- Claims outrun the evidence. Common scientific examples include converting
  association into causation, describing exploratory findings as validated,
  ignoring biological replication, or asserting clinical significance without
  supporting data.
- The author's voice or expertise changes abruptly. Vocabulary, sentence
  structure, confidence, spelling conventions, or technical sophistication
  differ markedly from prior work. More importantly, the writer cannot readily
  explain or defend specific choices in the text.
- Formulaic organization. Suspicious patterns include uniformly sized
  paragraphs, repeated three-part lists, excessive headings, symmetrical
  "benefits and challenges" treatment, and canned transitions such as
  "Moreover," "It is important to note," or "In conclusion."
- Repetition without advancement. The conclusion restates the introduction,
  adjacent paragraphs make the same point differently, and transitions create
  the appearance of progress without adding evidence.
- False precision or internal inconsistency. Exact percentages, dates,
  rankings, sample sizes, or technical terms appear without traceable sources,
  or conflict elsewhere in the document.

Where it helps, use the image generation tool to create explanatory and
illustrative, publication quality schematics and diagrams. Use it only when
available and authorized for the declared inputs. Check every label, arrow,
mechanism, and caption against the evidence. Never generate an experimental
image or data plot as if it were an observed result. If the tool is unavailable,
use an appropriate vector diagram or omit a nonessential illustration.

If writing a manuscript and generating data from theoretical or computational
models, create appropriate plots for the data and apply the proper statistics.
Generate plots in reproducible code from saved data. Label axes, units, sample
sizes, uncertainty, and the source of each figure. Respect the frozen analysis
plan and unit of analysis. Distinguish biological replicates, technical
replicates, and simulation repetitions; simulation runs are not biological
replication. Report effect sizes and justified uncertainty, test assumptions,
and account for multiple comparisons where applicable. Do not add significance
tests to deterministic outputs without a valid sampling model. A writer must
request missing analysis from the assigned analyst rather than recompute or
invent it. Report model assumptions and validation limits.

Before handing off, read the prose as a scientist encountering the study for
the first time. Does each paragraph advance the argument? Can the reader tell
what was done, what was found, why it matters, and what remains uncertain?
Revise the actual document, including captions, rather than merely reporting
that it needs polishing. Preserve claim IDs and scientific meaning. Reviewers
record exact passages and concrete corrections in their existing reports;
scientific blockers must map to their existing checklist. Editorial preferences
are nonblocking notes for the writer's existing revision, not a new review
role, approval step, or separate review loop.
